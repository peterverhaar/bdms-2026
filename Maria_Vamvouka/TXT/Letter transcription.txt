Letter from Leonard Woolf to John Lehmann (29/11/1940)
Author: Leonard Woolf
Recipient: John Lehmann

Monk’s House 	Rodmell 	29/11/40
Dear John, I do not wish, as I said, to get into a wrangle with you about this, but
I must at least point out this:
(1)	I showed your letter of 31 October to Virginia before I replied to you and
she also thought it rude, angry, and offensive and that it “was intended in the
sense” in which I read it. As regards my “advice”, which was so unhelpful, you did
in fact do exactly what I advised. I do not understand therefore what more I could
have done.
(2)	I had letters from you on October 15, 18, 23, 31 and on Nov. 1 and 5—six com-
munications in 21 days; from November 5 to November 27—22 days—you never wrote to
me one single line, though I twice wrote to you asking direct questions about the
Press. That was not the result of bad communications in the postal sense. From
Nov 6 to Nov 20 I was not even told the sales. During this period of silence Roger
Fry was apparently reprinted without any communication either to me or to V. who
had an important correction to make. We only learnt of the reprint by an ad.
If that is not being “cut off”, I do not know what would be.
(3)	I am in no “blissful illusion” about the difficulty of carrying on the Press
since September and as a matter of fact I expressed my appreciation of it and of 
what you had done since then when we last met and I agreed that there was no reason
why you should go every week to Letchworth. At the same interview we agreed that 
under the circumstances advertising must be left entirely in your hands, and that
was why I have been careful not to interfere with suggestions which would almost
certainly come to you too late. On two occasions when we met in town we discussed
the appearance of the books which were ready and I told you that I liked them. As
for Vita, Day Lewis and William, I have never even been shown a copy of any
of them, though I understand from the advertisements that they have already
been published.
	Your remark about making the Press a going concern, to which I took and take
exception, could not possibly refer to the period since September of this year.
(4)	I was compelled suddenly to alter my day from Friday to Wednesday this week
partly because I had to see my sister who is returning to Gambia and partly to
make arrangements for removal of furniture from M.S. I am afraid that I shall
not be able to come up at all next week as I am summoned to sit as a juror at
the Assizes in Lewes and have been refused exemption.

ANNOUNCEMENT
JOHN LANE THE BODLEY HEAD

Death in High Heels
CHRISTIANNA BRAND

      All through the year there flows across the silver doorstep
of Christophe et Cie a steady stream of shoes. Black, brown
and grey ; suède and canvas and kid ; the chic of Paris, the cut
of America, the solid worth of good English leather—tap,
tap, tap across the parquet. . .
      The satin slippers of the mannequins twist and turn ;
Rachel’s elegant court and Irene’s neat square toes fly over
the deep carpet ; there is a scutter of high blue heels, a
laughing aside, a disapproving glance from Mr. Bevan. His
sober Oxfords are planted firmly on the top step of the stair-
case and he keeps a watchful eye upon his staff ; Victoria and
Rachel are terribly irresponsible. He says as much to Doon
whose extravagant high-heeled sandals join his Oxfords upon
the stairs. There is a tenseness between them and, to cover
it, Doon launches a diatribe about a missing brooch. Her
inoffensive secretary shares with the charwoman their ready
suspicion.
      Two pairs of satin slippers, two of shabby black, elegant
court shoes, a pair of high blue heels, a pair of lace-ups, one
of sandals, a gent’s black Oxfords and a gent’s natty suèdes—
before the day is out one of these will kick and struggle in the
agony of death. Before the year is out another pair will dangle,
quietly at the end of a noose. But all through the year the
multi-coloured stream of shoes will continue to pass through
the silver portals in ever-increasing flow—tap, tap, tap
across the parquet. . . .
      Miss Brand is a new thriller writer and Death in High Heels
is an unusually excellent start to a promising career.
Crown 8vo. 75. 6d.

A Baker’s Dozen
LLEWELYN POWYS

      The late Llewelyn Powys’ work needs no introduction to
discriminating readers who follow the trend of modern
writing, and this volume of miscellaneous essays can only
heighten the reputation of one whose writing is acknowledged
to be closely related to the work of such masters as Gibbon,
Pater, Montaigne, Sir Thomas Browne and Charles Lamb.
      A Baker’s Dozen is a representative cross-section of Llewelyn
Powys’ work. His fresh and original philosophy, so evident in
Love and Death (his last published book) and in the exquisite
essay Glory of Life will be found again in these pages.
Many of the essays in this volume have already appeared in
English periodicals and the publishers now have pleasure in
presenting them for the first time in book form. This is the
first book of Llewelyn Powys’ to be published since his death
early this year
